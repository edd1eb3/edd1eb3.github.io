- Do not just open up your IDE and start typing right away. Take some time to conceptualize the problem and think through your approach,
whether it's old-school writing in a notebook or on a whiteboard.

There's a difference between understanding a problem logically and understanding the problem syntactically. You should begin with the
logic behind your project and then "translate" that logic to programming syntax.

- Another general tip is get used to working in blank canvases. What I mean by "black canvases" is a fresh sheet of paper or a clean
whiteboard. But a blank canvas can also be a notepad file. No matter if you write it on paper with pen, whiteboard with marker, or
typing it out in a blank notepad, the logic should be consistent and you should be able to write your pseudo-code very, very close to
what you're eventually gonna program because this helps you develop an approach without relying on AI features in your IDE, such as
auto-complete or auto-fill-suggestions.
You should be confident that something you type in a notepad, without any VS Code IntelliSense or whatever is correct, both logically
and syntactically. If you don't know what IntelliSense is, click this:</a> <a href="https://code.visualstudio.com/docs/editing/intellisense" target="_blank">
  How IntelliSense works
</a>
It's basically like word auto-complete on your phone when texting, except it's for auto-suggested code in the IDE. You should be comfortable
opening up a blank notepad and typing out different functions or whatever, like below, and be confident that it is correct, both logically
and syntatically, without having to compile or run it.

Back in the day, during the "Punch Card Era of Computing," programmers spent hours making sure every punchcard was correct. And if there were
an error, it would be hours of work to re-punch a new card. This was way before typing.</a> <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_programming_in_the_punched_card_era" target="_blank">
  Punchcard Era Wikipedia Page
</a>
And then when we first transitioned to the "Typing Era," there were still no fancy IDEs yet that would show a red squiggly line underneath
a typo or an error detected by IntelliSense. We have all these quick editing tools at our disposal now, but I do believe working in blank
canvases enables programmers to truly better understand programming concepts. When I was in school, tests in many courses had to be written
out by hand with no laptops or references. It may sound weird to the newer generation of programmers, but these oldschool methods expose
if you, as a programmer, truly understand the logic and syntax of your code.
